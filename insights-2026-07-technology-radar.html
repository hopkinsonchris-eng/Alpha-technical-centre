Technology Radar: July 2026

Six developments from June and July, ranked by evidence and by what they are worth to an asset owner. The one to act on is the cheapest: a Stanford and Petrobras team has shown that downhole temperature data you already have can measure fluid saturation directly, which changes how an in-place volume is argued.

Alpha Technical Centre Editorial
Houston, Texas · alpha-technical-centre.com

This is the first edition, so it covers two months rather than one. Six developments survived checking, out of roughly fifty screened.

A note on access, because it shapes what follows. Much of the SPE literature is member-only and OnePetro blocks automated access. Where a result sits behind that wall, this edition says so and reports only what is publicly readable. Every source below carries its tier and its access level, so you can see what you are relying on.

Temperature data you already have can measure saturation

Guimaraes, Galvao, Horne and Tartakovsky, working across Stanford and Petrobras, show that routinely acquired downhole temperature data allows direct estimation of fluid saturations, and that this improves in-place volume estimates in reservoirs with aquifer support1. The work is open access in SPE Journal.

The method treats a nonisothermal closed reservoir with impermeable boundaries at high Péclet number, builds a dimensionless formulation for an axisymmetric cylindrical domain, and derives analytical temperature expressions by the method of characteristics under pseudosteady pressure. The authors validate against commercial thermal simulators and wellbore data, then apply it to a field case with aquifer support1.

What it is good for: estimating stock-tank original oil in place without assuming a water saturation value. Pressure analysis on its own cannot separate saturation from volume, so the assumed water saturation carries the answer, and that assumption is one of the most common places an in-place number quietly goes wrong. Here the input is temperature already logged and sitting in the well file. There is nothing to acquire and nothing to buy.

For technical due diligence this is the most useful item in the edition. It attacks the assumption behind an in-place volume using data that already exists, which means it can be applied to a data room retrospectively rather than requiring a programme the seller has no reason to fund.

The honest limit: this is an analytical result with real assumptions attached, closed boundaries, high Péclet number, pseudosteady pressure, and one published field case. It is not yet a method with a track record across reservoir types. Treat it as a strong second opinion on a volumetric estimate, not a replacement for one.

The first closed-loop fracturing programme reaches the field

SPE 230613 reports the design and field deployment of what it describes as the first closed-loop hydraulic fracturing programme. The publicly visible portion sets out three layers, sensing, decision logic and execution, and states that the system runs fully autonomous fracturing operations, adjusting completion parameters in real time, with decision-making compressed from minutes or hours down to seconds2.

What it is good for, if it holds up: surface automation on fracturing spreads is already common, but it controls the pumps rather than the design. Closing the loop means the diagnostics change the treatment while the treatment is still running, instead of informing the next well. On a multi-well pad that is the difference between learning across a programme and learning within a stage.

The honest limit is larger than usual here, and it is worth stating plainly. The paper is behind the SPE member paywall and OnePetro refused automated access, so the authors, the operator, the basin, the well count and every result are unread. What is established is that a closed-loop programme has been designed and deployed and that a society-refereed paper describes it. The size of the gain is not established, at least not to anyone without a login.

A way to test what a vendor means by autonomous

de Wardt, with Cayeux and Mihai of NORCE, Macpherson of Baker Hughes, Pirovolou of Weatherford and Annaiyappa, propose a taxonomy separating automated drilling systems from autonomous ones, drawing definitions from aerospace, control theory and other high-risk industries3. The paper is open access in SPE Journal.

The framework scores ten traits: environment complexity, problem complexity, mission, risk management, dynamic planning, situational awareness, decision-making, execution, learning from experience and human-agent interaction. Each trait gets a quantification, so a claim of autonomy can be assessed rather than accepted. The authors also argue that an autonomous system can collaborate with human operators while keeping independent decision authority, which cuts against the assumption that autonomy means removing the human3.

What it is good for: procurement and diligence language. "Autonomous" is currently a marketing word in drilling, and a buyer evaluating a contractor or a technology has had no agreed way to test it. Ten scored traits give you a checklist to put in front of a vendor, and a basis for comparing two bids that both claim the same thing. It costs nothing to adopt.

The limit: this is a framework, not a field result. It changes how you assess claims. It does not drill anything.

EOR is back on the society's agenda, and the results are paywalled

The June issue of JPT gave its EOR technology focus to three papers, in a section edited by Kristian Mogensen of ADNOC. OTC 36263 reports encapsulated polymer technology overcoming shear degradation and injectivity loss offshore. SPE 230249 reports a test of the validity of in-situ combustion in the Permian Basin. SPE 230250 reports a gas and liquid coinjection process entering the Permian6.

These are titles from a verified table of contents. The synopses are member-restricted and the papers sit on OnePetro, so nothing here has been read beyond the title. That is why this is one item rather than three, and why no result is quoted.

Why the direction still deserves attention: shear degradation and injectivity loss are the two failure modes that have killed most offshore polymer floods, so a paper claiming to address both is worth pulling. In-situ combustion has a long history of promising laboratory work and disappointing field application, which makes a validity test in a basin as heavily instrumented as the Permian interesting whichever way it came out. If you have an SPE login, these are the three to read from the June issue.

Alberta funds ten drilling technologies, two of them for brownfields

Emissions Reduction Alberta awarded 37 million dollars across ten drilling technology projects worth about 179 million dollars combined. Most went to geothermal: 8 million dollars each to Eavor Technologies for superhot resource access and Rodatherm Energy for closed-loop geothermal power, and 4.4 million to Borobotics for an autonomous electric drilling robot5.

Two smaller awards matter more for mature oil and gas. Global Dynamics received 2.25 million dollars for multilateral well re-entry, and Weatherford Canada 1.42 million for real-time sensing and ranging in SAGD drilling. Phase Advanced Sensor Systems received 420,000 dollars for high-temperature downhole pressure sensors and Precision Drilling 3.1 million for robotic pipe handling5.

What it is good for: multilateral re-entry is a redevelopment tool. It reaches new drainage from a wellbore that already exists, avoiding a new surface slot and a new well cost, and that arithmetic decides whether a mature field gets a second phase or gets abandoned. High-temperature pressure sensors matter for the same reason, since thermal and deep completions are where instrumentation fails first.

The limit: this is funding, not results. Ten projects have money, none has a published field outcome, and public innovation programmes convert poorly. Treat it as a two to three year watch list.

Depleted gas reservoirs get a second life as storage

Harbour Energy's Viking CCS project has cleared front-end engineering and design and had its development funding approved. The numbers are specific enough to be useful: eight suitable depleted gas reservoirs in the Viking fields, injection at roughly 9,000 ft beneath a salt layer 600 to 1,000 ft thick, 400 million tonnes of storage capacity, an initial target of up to 4 million tonnes a year in the early 2030s, and a subsea pipeline sized for up to 30 million tonnes a year against an expansion case of 10. Investment required is put at more than 13 billion pounds4.

What it is good for: the reservoir engineering that makes a depleted gas field a storage site is the same work as managing it in late life. Pressure history, seal integrity, well stock and abandonment status are the inputs either way. For anyone holding a mature gas asset near industrial CO2, that is a second exit route to price alongside abandonment, and these figures give a reference point for scale.

The limit: this is trade reporting on a company announcement, not a technical paper, so it is the weakest evidence here and marked as such in the table. First operations are early 2030s, and 13 billion pounds is a plan rather than a spend.

What we would use, and where

The temperature work goes into use immediately, on due diligence and redevelopment screening. It costs nothing to try, the data is already in the well files, and it bears on the number that decides the valuation. We would run it alongside a conventional volumetric and treat any disagreement between the two as the thing worth investigating.

The autonomous drilling taxonomy goes into procurement language this quarter. Ten scored traits are a better question to put to a drilling contractor than asking whether the system is autonomous. Multilateral re-entry goes on the redevelopment watch list, to be revisited when the Alberta projects publish results rather than on the strength of a grant.

Closed-loop fracturing and the three EOR papers we cannot assess. The direction of both looks right and the evidence is not reachable without a society login. That is a gap rather than a judgement, and it is the first thing we would fix about this edition.

Method. Window: June and July 2026, widened from one month because this is the first edition. Roughly 50 items were screened across the SPE and JPT June issue and July news, OnePetro, and targeted searches of SEG, EAGE and adjacent literature. Six are reported. Twelve were rejected as company or market news rather than technology, fifteen as outside the window, and two because no readable primary content could be reached. OnePetro returns HTTP 403 to automated access and JPT technology-focus synopses are member-restricted, so several results are cited at title level with the access level marked. Seismic acquisition and interpretation, geological modelling, well integrity, transport, trading and emissions measurement produced nothing in window and are recorded as gaps rather than as quiet fields. The Middle East, Asia-Pacific and Africa produced nothing, which is a search limitation this series intends to fix. The full audit trail, including every item screened and the impact scoring, is in the repository dossier for this edition.
